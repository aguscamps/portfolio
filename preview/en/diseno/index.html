01

Improntus · Client Sparta, Chile

Five brands, five identities, one system

UI kits as the single source of truth between design and development: components defined once, variants resolved per brand, for five stores that look distinct.

Context
Sparta runs the sports retail operation of five brands in Chile — Sparta, New Balance, Head, Speedo and Trek. Each has its own identity, audience and commercial calendar, but all of them run on a shared technical base.
The problem
Designing five stores that look distinct from one another, each true to its own brand guidelines, without the shared base forcing everything to be reinvented every time or letting them drift into looking alike.
What I did
For New Balance Chile I designed the complete interface in Figma; for Sparta I designed part of the pages. I built the UI kits for the stores, which became the single source of truth between design and development: components are defined once and brand variants resolved on top, instead of redesigning screen by screen. I then built both themes myself, so the design was validated against the real implementation rather than against a mockup.
Outcome
Two stores in production with clearly distinct identities, and a component system that holds all five together.

02

Pago TIC · Payments fintech · 2020–2021

The design system of a fintech

The company's design system and everything built on it: checkout, back office, landing pages, mailings and the rename campaign artwork.

Context
PayperTIC is a payments platform. I was on the design team, responsible for the graphic and interface design of the whole company.
The problem
The screens had been growing without a common criterion: every landing page, every form and every backoffice screen solved the same things a different way.
What I did
I built the company's design system and designed on top of it: the checkout of the payments platform, the backoffice and frontoffice screens, the campaign landing pages with forms, and the banners and social pieces. The landing pages, forms, banners and mailings I marked up myself in HTML and CSS; the rest of the frontend was handled by the development team. I also did motion graphics: promotional videos and internal training pieces.
The merger
Towards the end of my time there, PayperTIC merged with +Simple and the company was renamed Pago TIC. I designed the pieces for the renaming campaign, including the transition from one logo to the other animated in After Effects, which is the video alongside.

03

Inmental Interactive · UNICEF · 2016–2020

Donating from a phone, without friction

A mobile-first donation flow of few steps, with preset amounts and recurring giving offered within the same step, not afterwards.

Context
UNICEF's fundraising and awareness site. Most of the traffic arrived from phones, driven by campaigns.
The problem
Donating is a decision that cools off fast: every extra field, every extra screen and every doubt about where the money goes costs donors.
What I did
I designed the site mobile first, with a simple, transparent payment flow: preset amounts, so the user does not have to decide a figure from scratch, and recurring donation offered within the same step rather than afterwards. I also designed the social and mailing campaigns that drove traffic to the site, and a downloadable, printable activity book given to donors as a gift.
Outcome
A donation path of few steps, readable on a small screen, with recurrence built into the flow.

04

Inmental Interactive · Multishan, Spain · 2020

A trade fair without the fair

Walkable halls with spatial navigation instead of a menu, to keep the logic of walking a trade fair that could no longer be visited.

Context
Multishan ran in-person exhibitions in Spain. The pandemic cancelled all of them, and the entire business with them.
The problem
Moving to a screen something whose value was walking the place: wandering, stopping where something catches your eye and talking to the brand.
What I did
I designed a virtual exhibition platform with walkable halls: the visitor moves through the space and each brand's stand opens from interactive points spread across the hall. The navigation is spatial rather than a menu, to keep the logic of walking through. I also designed the interface of their fast-food self-service terminal.
Outcome
A working replacement for the in-person event during the period when there was no alternative.

How I work

On most projects, the point where quality is lost is the handoff between design and development: questions left unanswered, decisions resolved by default and detail trimmed to hit the date. I cover both stages, so that handoff does not exist. In practice, this is what changes:

I translate the brief, I do not transcribe it

I take part in the client meetings and turn what they ask for — which often arrives vague or contradictory — into something concrete and bounded. That translation is half the design work and is usually done badly, or not at all.

I design knowing what it costs to build

When I put an interface together in Figma I know how much work sits behind each decision and which ones will get complicated on the platform. That lets me push where the detail changes something and give way where it does not, and it means what I hand over gets built as it is, without last-minute cuts.

I work in components, not screens

The UI kit becomes the single source of truth between design and development: components are defined once and variants resolved on top of that base. That way the system scales across several brands without redesigning screen by screen.

I review on the device, not in the mockup

An interface fails at the intermediate breakpoint, with a product name longer than in the mockup, in the older browser. That is what I test before calling anything finished, on a real screen and not by shrinking a window.
