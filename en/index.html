01

Improntus · Client: Sparta, Chile

Five stores, one codebase

Five brands with their own identity and their own commercial calendar, on a shared Adobe Commerce codebase, with a single person on frontend.

Context
Sparta runs the sports retail of five brands in Chile —Sparta, New Balance, Head, Speedo and Trek—, each with its own store, identity and commercial calendar, on a shared Adobe Commerce codebase.
The problem
Two of the stores needed a new theme and the other three were still running the theme inherited from the previous agency. Anything touched in the shared codebase —an improvement, a module, an infrastructure change— had to keep working in both worlds at once.
What I did
I took ownership of the frontend for all five stores. For New Balance Chile I designed the entire interface in Figma and built the theme from scratch; for Sparta I took part in the page design and also built the theme from scratch. On Head, Speedo and Trek I worked on the inherited theme: adapting the code to the site's new infrastructure, fixing pre-existing issues and rolling out the improvements and modules shared across every store. I also built the UI kits that became the single source of truth between design and development.
Outcome
Two new sites in production with distinct identities, and three inherited ones kept running and up to date without a rebuild. Five stores in parallel, with one person on frontend.

02

Improntus · Internal project · No public material

The agency's base theme

A base theme of our own to replace the platform's, leading a team of three frontends, so that no new project starts by taking the default theme apart.

Context
Every Magento project starts from the platform's default theme, built to demo rather than to ship. Each new store began by taking it apart.
The problem
The frontend team repeated the same groundwork on every project and tripped over the same native platform bugs, project after project.
What I did
I built the agency's own base theme to replace it, aimed at cutting markup time for the whole team: I simplified the variable system, brought in Grunt webfonts for the custom icon typefaces and fixed at the root the native bugs we had been patching one by one. I led the project with a team of three frontend developers: I set the scope, created and distributed the tasks by sprint in Jira, explained the goals and reviewed every delivery, while taking markup tasks in parallel.
Outcome
A shared base adopted by the team, with less start-up time per project and the known bugs solved up front.

03

Improntus · Internal project · No public material

42 audits, one standard

I set the scope, split the work across the team and unified the criteria of 42 frontend audits that backed the sales team's proposals.

Context
The sales team needed technical arguments on two fronts: convincing prospects and selling improvements to the clients we already had.
The problem
Auditing dozens of sites is beyond one person, and the reports had to come out to a single standard: if each one measures different things, they are no use for comparing or for backing a proposal in front of a client.
What I did
I defined the scope of the audit —performance, responsive, accessibility and code quality— and took the coordination: I kept track of them in a spreadsheet, distributed the audits across the team, reviewed and edited every delivery so they all spoke the same language, and escalated them to management. I ran a good share of them myself.
Outcome
42 audits: 18 for prospects and 24 for the existing client base. Among the prospects were Sparta and Granado, which later became clients of the agency; the Sparta ecosystem ended up being mine to own.

05

Inmental Interactive · UNICEF · 2016–2020

Donating from a phone, without friction

A mobile-first donation flow of few steps, with preset amounts and recurring giving built into the same step rather than offered afterwards.

Context
UNICEF's fundraising and awareness site. Most of the traffic arrived from phones, driven by campaigns.
The problem
Donating is a decision that cools off fast: every extra field, every extra screen and every doubt about where the money goes costs donors.
What I did
I designed the site mobile first, with a simple, transparent payment flow: preset amounts so the user doesn't have to pick a figure from scratch, and the option of a recurring donation in that same step. I also designed the social and mailing campaigns that drove traffic to the site, and a downloadable, printable activity book given to donors as a gift.
Outcome
A donation path of few steps, readable on a small screen, with recurrence built into the flow instead of offered afterwards.

How I work

On most projects, the point where quality is lost is the handoff between design and development: questions that go unanswered, decisions settled by default and detail trimmed to make the date. I am a designer and a developer specialising in Adobe Commerce: I cover both stages, so that handoff doesn't exist. Here is what that changes in practice:

I design knowing what it costs to build

When I put an interface together in Figma I know how much work sits behind each decision and which ones will fight the platform. That lets me push where the detail changes something and give way where it doesn't, and it means what I hand over gets built as it is, with no last-minute cuts.

I build understanding the intent

No design covers everything: the error screen is missing, the product name is longer than in the mockup, a case nobody foresaw shows up. I close those gaps by following the logic of the design, instead of inventing something that clashes or stopping the project to ask.

I test where stores break

A store breaks at the in-between breakpoint, in the old browser, in the cart with fifteen items, in the image that loads late and shifts the content. That is what I test before calling something done, and I measure it with Lighthouse: in a store, the Core Web Vitals that suffer most are LCP and CLS.

I build my own tooling

I write my own agents and skills in Claude Code to translate Figma designs into code, audit and document. They carry the project's conventions —naming, file structure, semantics and accessibility— so what they produce meets good practice up front instead of being fixed afterwards. It is time taken from the repetitive part and given back to the part that needs judgement.
